A. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
================================================================================
PiProbe's central idea - a Raspberry Pi wired "truth" anchor plus several
cheap ESP32 Wi-Fi probes, feeding a calibrated cross-layer fault classifier
that separates Wi-Fi, LAN, WAN/ISP, DNS, and probe-health problems - is
publishable, but NOT because of the dashboard, the MQTT pipeline, or the
low hardware cost. Those are 2014-era solved problems (Wi-Pi, UNINETT,
CheesePi, Netrics, Internet-Pi all did low-cost Pi-based distributed
Wi-Fi/broadband measurement with time-series dashboards, years ago, and
several are still-maintained open-source projects). Reviewers in 2027 will
recognize the architecture pattern on sight and will not award novelty for it.

What is NOT yet solved, and what a rigorous PiProbe paper can credibly claim,
is narrower and more specific:

  1. A validated, calibrated mapping from cheap-microcontroller measurements
     (ESP32 RSSI, local RTT/jitter/loss, WAN latency/DNS/HTTP) to a labeled,
     multi-class fault taxonomy, with reported measurement uncertainty and
     confidence intervals - not just raw signal collection. Most prior
     low-cost Pi/ESP32 systems (Wi-Pi, Netrics, CheesePi, Internet-Pi,
     WiFiMon) measure and visualize; they do not calibrate RSSI against
     ground truth or attach confidence to a diagnosis.
  2. A controlled, repeatable fault-injection protocol run on real household/
     building infrastructure (not a Faraday-cage lab testbed) with ground-
     truth labels, enabling supervised and rule-based baselines to be
     compared head-to-head with reported false-ISP-alarm rate and detection
     delay - metrics almost nobody in your comparison set reports.
  3. Explicit treatment of "probe fault" as its own diagnostic class (the
     measuring instrument can itself fail - power loss, clock drift, Wi-Fi
     disassociation) - a category that is conspicuously absent from every
     comparable system found, including the very recent WITNESS/"Toward
     Realistic Wi-Fi Fault Diagnosis" benchmark (2026).
  4. An honest, quantified account of ESP32 measurement limitations
     (board-to-board RSSI variance, enclosure/antenna effects, 1-antenna
     ESP32-C5 ceiling on Wi-Fi 6 gains) feeding directly into the model's
     reported uncertainty, rather than hidden in a "future work" line.

What is already solved and must NOT be claimed as a contribution: low-cost
Pi-based Wi-Fi/broadband monitoring (solved since 2014); MQTT+InfluxDB/
SQLite+Grafana telemetry pipelines (standard, commodity engineering, used by
at least four systems below); room-level/indoor Wi-Fi heatmapping from
RSSI (solved, including specifically with ESP32s in monitor mode); separating
"is it Wi-Fi or is it the ISP" at a basic level (yslow, a free CLI tool,
already does this from a single host); and SHAP-style explainability applied
to black-box fault classifiers (a mature, borrowed technique from predictive-
maintenance and telecom RCA literature, not novel in itself).

The single biggest competitive risk is a dataset paper that appears to have
been published within the last few months of this writing: "Toward Realistic
Wi-Fi Fault Diagnosis: A Multi-Modal Benchmark" and its accompanying WITNESS
dataset (Zhang et al., Central South University / Tohoku University,
arXiv 2026, IEEE DataPort). It does almost exactly what Sections 6-9 of your
brief ask for - automated fault injection, >10,000 labeled samples, a unified
benchmark across detection/classification/localization, even an LLM-based
explainability evaluation - on a real (if campus-scale, not residential)
Wi-Fi testbed. PiProbe MUST cite this paper and differentiate explicitly
(see Section D) or a reviewer will cite it for you, unfavorably.

India-specific deployment: by itself, close to zero added research value as a
headline claim - Wi-Pi itself was deployed and evaluated at IIT Hyderabad in
2014, so "built and tested in India" is not new. It becomes a real (modest)
contribution only if reframed as infrastructure/ISP diversity: nearly every
comparable system was deployed in the US, EU, or China (Netrics-Chicago,
CheesePi-Sweden, WiFiMon-Europe, RAID-France, WITNESS-China); documenting how
a different last-mile/ISP/power environment changes fault distributions or
probe reliability is a legitimate, citable, small dataset-diversity
contribution. "India" as a flag on the title is not; "cross-infrastructure
generalization including a non-Western residential ISP environment" as a
stated limitation-turned-contribution is.

Bottom line (expanded in Section M): the core idea clears the publishability
bar, but the project as described reads as still in the design phase - no
pilot dataset, no completed fault-injection trials are mentioned in your
brief. Recommendation: CONTINUE EXPERIMENTS, not submit now, and treat the
I2MTC 2027 full-paper deadline (Dec 4, 2026 - see Section 8) as realistic
ONLY if hardware and a pilot data pass are already substantially underway
outside this conversation.

8. RAID: Root cause Anomaly Identification and Diagnosis [VERIFIED - full
   text obtained; matches your list, you had the right name]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Year/Venue    : 2025, ECML PKDD (European Conference on Machine Learning and
                Principles and Practice of Knowledge Discovery in Databases),
                Porto, Portugal, pp. 438-455, DOI
                10.1007/978-3-662-72243-5_25, HAL id hal-05219367
Authors       : Joel Roman Ky, Bertrand Mathieu, Abdelkader Lahmadi, Minqi
                Wang, Nicolas Marrot, Raouf Boutaba (Universite de Lorraine/
                CNRS/Inria/LORIA, University of Luxembourg, Orange
                Innovation, University of Waterloo)
Platform      : Controlled lab testbed using Faraday cages, RF attenuators,
                a commercial "Livebox" (Orange's home gateway/AP), an NVIDIA
                CloudXR server, and a Meta Quest 2 - NOT low-cost distributed
                ESP32/Pi probes; this is carrier-operator-grade lab
                equipment, closer to "WAN/vendor lab" than "consumer home."
Measurements  : 112 time-series KPI features pulled from the access point
                every 3 seconds (RSSI/RSRP, airtime/channel utilization,
                retry rates, MCS/bitrate stats), application-level Cloud-VR
                QoS metrics, raw packet captures.
Diagnosis     : Two-stage ML pipeline: (1) contrastive-learning anomaly
                detector (built on the authors' own prior "CATS" method) that
                needs NO labels for the normal-vs-anomalous step, then (2) a
                lightweight supervised classifier (SVM/logistic regression)
                to name the specific impairment. Three classes evaluated:
                Normal, Coverage (signal attenuation), Interference
                (competing traffic).
Dataset       : 13,657 labeled time-series windows (9,524 train / 4,133
                test), Cloud-VR-specific; public code at
                https://github.com/joelromanky/raid (dataset availability
                not separately confirmed in this pass - check the repo).
Reported perf.: Accuracy 81.83% (+/-2.96), F1 81.60% (+/-3.05), beating
                1-NN-DTW, T-Loss, TS2Vec, TS-TCC, iForest, USAD, and SimCLR
                baselines on their own benchmark; authors themselves report a
                real weakness - the model struggles to separate "Coverage"
                from "Normal" (many Coverage windows misclassified as
                Normal), i.e. the hardest, most PiProbe-relevant fault class
                (gradual signal degradation) is exactly where RAID is
                weakest.
Limitation    : Only 3 impairment classes (no LAN/DNS/HTTP/probe-health, no
                ISP-outage class); evaluated in a single Cloud-VR use case on
                operator-grade hardware in Faraday cages - the opposite
                deployment regime from PiProbe's "cheap and in a real
                building" goal; authors explicitly list "home networks where
                multiple sources of impairment coexist" as their OWN stated
                future work.
PiProbe diff. : PiProbe operates in exactly the messier, uncontrolled,
                multi-impairment real-home/building regime RAID's authors
                say they have NOT yet done. PiProbe's taxonomy is wider
                (adds WAN/DNS/HTTP/probe faults entirely absent from RAID),
                and its hardware is two orders of magnitude cheaper than a
                Livebox + CloudXR rig. PiProbe should cite RAID as the
                closest recent ML methodology precedent and as the source of
                a directly reusable evaluation-metric vocabulary (weighted P/
                R/F1, accuracy, "normalized accuracy") while being explicit
                that it targets a different (harder-to-control, more
                realistic) deployment regime.
Link          : https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/66323/1/preprint_ecml_pkdd_2025_ads_265%20%281%29.pdf
                https://github.com/joelromanky/raid

D. RESEARCH-GAP STATEMENT
================================================================================
Established by this literature review, 2014-2026, prioritizing 2020-2026:

WHAT IS ALREADY SOLVED (do not re-claim):
  - Low-cost, distributed, Pi-based Wi-Fi/broadband measurement with
    time-series dashboards (Wi-Pi 2014; UNINETT; CheesePi 2017; Netrics
    2021-present; Internet-Pi, ongoing).
  - Basic Wi-Fi-vs-LAN-vs-WAN layer separation for troubleshooting (WiSlow/
    DYSWIS 2014; even a free CLI tool, yslow, does this today from one host).
  - ML-based classification of Wi-Fi interference/impairment causes on
    research or carrier-grade testbeds (Wi-Dia 2018; RAID 2025; WITNESS/
    multi-modal benchmark 2026).
  - Open, labeled, multi-modal Wi-Fi fault datasets with automated injection
    (WITNESS, 2026 - this is very recent and directly competing).
  - Indoor Wi-Fi radio/heat mapping, including specifically with ESP32s in
    monitor mode (IEEE Sensors Journal paper, A-WEAR/ORIENTATE-funded).
  - SHAP-based explainability for fault diagnosis generally, including in
    telecom/mobile-network RCA specifically (multiple reviews; a published
    SHAP-based mobile-network RCA clustering paper).

WHAT IS NOT YET SOLVED (credible gaps):
  - A fault-diagnosis system that is simultaneously (a) WAN/ISP/DNS/HTTP-
    inclusive (not Wi-Fi-only, unlike WITNESS and Wi-Dia), (b) built on
    sub-$15 commodity microcontroller probes (not Livebox/CloudXR-grade
    like RAID, not campus-AP-grade like WITNESS), and (c) evaluated with a
    reported, calibrated measurement-uncertainty budget rather than only a
    point-estimate accuracy.
  - Explicit treatment of probe/instrument self-failure as a diagnostic
    class, so the system does not silently blame the ISP for its own dead
    battery.
  - Controlled fault injection carried out in real, uncontrolled-RF-
    environment buildings (not a Faraday cage, not a dedicated lab AP-STA
    tree) with reported false-ISP-alarm rate and detection delay - these
    two specific metrics, which your brief names explicitly, are essentially
    absent from the systems found in this search.
  - Any dataset or system in this search deployed and characterized under a
    South/South-Asian residential ISP and power environment (every
    comparator is US/EU/China) - a legitimate, if secondary, generalization
    gap.

WHICH PROPOSED PIPROBE FEATURES ARE NOT NOVEL (repeat, for emphasis - see
Section C for full detail): the hardware architecture pattern, the
MQTT/InfluxDB/Grafana pipeline, room-level heat-mapping, basic Wi-Fi/LAN/WAN
layer separation as an idea, and SHAP as a technique are all pre-existing.

WHICH CLAIMS WOULD BE REJECTED AS EXAGGERATED if made in a submitted paper:
  - "First system to distinguish Wi-Fi, LAN, and WAN faults" - false by 11
    years (WiSlow/DYSWIS, 2014).
  - "First open Wi-Fi fault-diagnosis dataset" - false by however many
    months separate PiProbe's submission from WITNESS's 2026 release.
  - "First ML-based Wi-Fi fault classifier" - false (Wi-Dia 2018, RAID 2025,
    WITNESS's benchmark 2026).
  - "Novel distributed low-cost Wi-Fi probe architecture" - false as stated;
    Wi-Pi, Netrics, CheesePi, Internet-Pi all precede it.
  - Any claim that frames the MQTT/Grafana/InfluxDB pipeline itself as a
    contribution.
  - Any claim that geographic deployment in India is itself a research
    contribution, unstated and unmeasured.

IS CROSS-LAYER DIAGNOSIS USING LOW-COST COMMODITY PROBES GENUINELY
PUBLISHABLE? Yes, conditionally. The problem (cross-layer Wi-Fi/LAN/WAN/DNS
diagnosis) is old; the specific combination of commodity-microcontroller
hardware, explicit calibration/uncertainty reporting, controlled injection
in realistic (non-lab, non-campus) buildings, and a probe-self-fault class
is not yet published together anywhere found in this search. This is an
INCREMENTAL, MEASUREMENT-SCIENCE-FLAVORED contribution appropriate for a
venue like IEEE I2MTC (whose own scope is explicitly measurement
methodology and calibration, not networking novelty for its own sake) -
not a systems/networking breakthrough appropriate for, say, SIGCOMM or
NSDI. Match the ambition of the claims to I2MTC's actual bar, which rewards

E. FINAL RESEARCH QUESTIONS AND HYPOTHESES
================================================================================
Problem statement (final): Low-cost distributed Wi-Fi probes can collect
cross-layer network telemetry cheaply, but it is not established whether
such commodity-microcontroller measurements can be calibrated and combined
with wired-baseline and WAN-layer signals to produce a fault diagnosis that
is both accurate AND trustworthy (i.e., accompanied by a defensible
uncertainty estimate) across Wi-Fi, LAN, WAN/ISP, DNS, and probe-health
fault classes, in real (not lab-controlled) indoor RF environments.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
RQ1 (primary): Can a calibrated, low-cost ESP32-probe-plus-Pi-anchor
architecture achieve fault-classification accuracy (macro-F1) comparable to
published ML Wi-Fi root-cause systems (e.g., RAID's ~82% accuracy / ~81.6%
F1 on a 3-class problem) across a WIDER fault taxonomy (Wi-Fi + LAN + WAN +
DNS + probe-health, 7+ classes)?
  H1: A cross-layer feature set (Wi-Fi PHY + local RTT/jitter/loss + WAN
      latency/DNS/HTTP) achieves significantly higher macro-F1 than any
      single-layer subset (Wi-Fi-only, or WAN-only) baseline, at p<0.05
      over repeated trials, with the effect size (F1 delta) reported
      alongside significance.
  Falsifiable null: cross-layer fusion gives no significant F1 improvement
      over the best single-layer baseline (a real, scientifically useful
      possible outcome - WITNESS's own headline finding is that leveraging
      heterogeneous data remains hard, so H1's null is a live possibility,
      not a strawman).

RQ2: Does per-device RSSI calibration (correcting for board-to-board
offset and enclosure/antenna attenuation) measurably reduce misclassification
of coverage-type faults, the class every comparator system (notably RAID)
finds hardest?
  H2: Per-device-calibrated RSSI reduces the Coverage<->Normal confusion-
      matrix off-diagonal mass by a significant margin versus uncalibrated
      raw RSSI, holding the rest of the pipeline fixed (an ablation, not a
      new model).

RQ3: Does the diagnosis model generalize across rooms, time-of-day/week, and
(if a second building is included - see Section 7/M) across buildings,
without significant accuracy loss, or is it overfit to its training
deployment?
  H3: Cross-room and cross-time macro-F1 will be measurably lower than
      in-distribution (same-room, same-time-window) macro-F1 - this is
      EXPECTED to show some degradation; the research question is how much,
      and whether calibration (H2) narrows that gap. Report both numbers
      plainly even if generalization is weak; a negative or mixed result
      here is still a valid, reportable finding given your brief's emphasis
      on rigor over a flattering headline number.

RQ4: Can the system explicitly separate "probe fault" from "ISP fault" with
acceptably low false-ISP-alarm rate, i.e., does adding a probe-health class
reduce the rate at which a dead/disconnected probe is misreported as an
ISP outage, relative to a system without that class?
  H4: Adding an explicit probe-health class to the taxonomy significantly
      lowers the false-ISP-alarm rate compared to an ablated model trained
      without that class (where probe failures are forced into the nearest
      other category).

RQ5 (secondary/stretch - only if time allows, see Section 7 scope
recommendation): Does a SHAP-based explanation of the classifier's decisions
remain faithful (e.g., via a held-out "known ground truth cause" check) when
the top contributing features are compared against the actual injected fault
type, and is this explanation substantially more useful to a non-expert than
a simple rule-based "if RSSI<X then coverage fault" decision tree?
  H5: SHAP's top-ranked feature(s) match the actual injected fault's
      most physically relevant signal (e.g., RSSI for Coverage, WAN RTT for
      ISP-link faults) in a majority of correctly-classified cases,
      measured against a predefined feature-to-fault-class relevance map
      prepared before evaluation (to avoid post-hoc rationalization).

F. EXPERIMENTAL PROTOCOL
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
F.1 RECOMMENDED FAULT TAXONOMY (for the first paper - see Section 7 for what
to postpone)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WI-FI COVERAGE        - weak signal due to distance/walls; induced via
                            physical probe relocation and/or attenuation
                            (foil/enclosure shielding - RAID uses RF
                            attenuators and Faraday cages; PiProbe's
                            lower-budget equivalent is physical distance
                            and known-attenuation materials, reported as a
                            limitation versus lab-grade RF attenuators).
2. WI-FI INTERFERENCE    - co-channel/adjacent-channel Wi-Fi contention
                            (a second AP broadcasting on an overlapping
                            channel) and non-Wi-Fi 2.4GHz interference
                            (e.g., microwave oven, Bluetooth burst traffic)
                            - precedent: Wi-Dia explicitly targets this
                            class; RAID's "Interference" class uses a
                            competing-station traffic generator, directly
                            reusable as a methodological template.
3. WI-FI MAC/CONGESTION  - high client density / channel-utilization
                            saturation on the home AP; induced with
                            synthetic competing traffic from additional
                            stations.
4. LAN/ROUTER FAULT      - router CPU overload, DHCP exhaustion, bad
                            cable/switch port on the wired segment; this
                            is where the Pi's clean wired baseline earns
                            its place in the architecture - it is the
                            one vantage point immune to Wi-Fi-layer noise,
                            so LAN-vs-not-LAN should be the taxonomy's most
                            reliable split.
5. WAN/ISP FAULT         - injected via a controlled upstream
                            shaping/dropping device (e.g., tc/netem on a
                            router-adjacent Linux box, or a managed switch/
                            travel router positioned between the home
                            router and the ISP modem) rather than by
                            actually disrupting the live ISP link -
                            ETHICAL/PRACTICAL NOTE: do not deliberately
                            degrade a production ISP connection relied on
                            by a household for real use; inject WAN
                            impairment on an isolated test path or VLAN.
6. DNS FAULT              - induced resolver latency or resolver failure
                            (point probes at a deliberately slow/unreachable
                            resolver for trial windows), isolated from
                            general WAN latency so the model must learn to
                            tell them apart - this split is explicitly
                            named in your brief and is NOT separately
                            evaluated in any comparator system found.
7. PROBE FAULT            - power loss, clock desync beyond tolerance,
                            probe-side Wi-Fi disassociation; induced by
                            deliberately power-cycling or disconnecting
                            individual ESP32 probes during trials. This is
                            PiProbe's most distinctive taxonomy class (see
                            Section C/D) - do not under-resource its
                            trial count relative to the "flashier" classes.
8. NORMAL / NO-FAULT      - baseline operating condition, collected in
                            substantially larger volume than any single
                            fault class (see F.4).

Explicitly recommended for POSTPONEMENT, not inclusion, in the first paper
(see Section 7 for the full scope argument): application/HTTP-server-side
faults (CDN slowness, origin server issues) - these are confounded with WAN
faults using only client-side probes and need a controlled server-side
testbed to inject cleanly; cellular/5G measurements - out of scope entirely
per your own brief.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
F.2 CONTROLLED FAULT-INJECTION PROCEDURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Baseline capture: for each room/location, collect >= 60 minutes of
   NORMAL-condition telemetry from every probe before any fault is injected
   that day, to characterize that room's idle noise floor.
2. One fault class injected per trial window (no overlapping faults within
   a single trial, to keep ground-truth labels unambiguous) - multi-fault
   trials are a valid STRETCH addition later (see Section 7) but should not
   be in the first paper's primary dataset, to avoid label ambiguity
   undermining everything else.
3. Each trial: fixed duration (e.g., 5-10 minutes), fixed room, logged
   start/end timestamps on the SAME clock reference used by the probes
   (see F.5 on time sync), with the injection method and its parameters
   (e.g., attenuation material + estimated dB loss, netem loss percentage,
   competing-traffic bitrate) recorded in a structured injection log - this
   log IS the ground-truth label source (see F.3).
4. Randomize trial ORDER across fault classes and rooms within a data-
   collection day, to avoid confounding "time of day" or "day of week" with
   "fault class" (a classic and easy-to-miss leakage/confound risk a
   reviewer will probe for).
5. Minimum trial count per (fault class x room) cell: 20-30 independent
   trials, to support confidence intervals and the statistical testing H1-H4
   require; fewer than this will not support claims with p-values a
   reviewer will accept.
6. Safety/ethics: WAN and DNS fault injection must run on an isolated test
   path (dedicated VLAN, travel router, or netem on a Linux box positioned
   between the Pi and the real ISP modem) so that real household internet
   use is never actually disrupted; log this isolation explicitly in the
   paper's methodology section, since a reviewer WILL ask how you avoided
   degrading someone's real internet access to generate WAN-fault samples.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
F.3 GROUND-TRUTH LABELING METHOD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Ground truth is the injection log itself (fault class + start/end timestamp
+ injection parameters), NOT a post-hoc human judgment call - this is a
methodological strength to state explicitly, since several comparator
papers (e.g., WiSlow/DYSWIS-era crowdsourced systems) rely on less precise,
user-reported ground truth. Label each telemetry sample/window with the
fault class active during its timestamp range per the injection log; samples
collected during the injection device's own warm-up/cool-down transition
(e.g., first/last 5-10 seconds of a netem rule taking effect) should be
EXCLUDED from the labeled dataset rather than mislabeled, and this
exclusion window should itself be reported as a methodological parameter.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
F.4 CROSS-ROOM, CROSS-TIME, CROSS-DEVICE, CROSS-BUILDING VALIDATION DESIGN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Cross-room: hold out all trials from one or more ENTIRE rooms from
    training; test on the held-out room(s). Tests spatial generalization.
  - Cross-time: hold out an entire contiguous time block (e.g., the final
    data-collection week) from training; test on it. Tests temporal
    generalization / drift (relevant because home Wi-Fi/ISP conditions
    genuinely change over weeks - new neighboring networks appear, ISP
    congestion patterns shift).
  - Cross-device: swap which physical ESP32 units are "seen" in training
    vs. test, to isolate whether the model has learned device-specific
    quirks (reinforces the board-to-board calibration question in RQ2/H2).
  - Cross-building: only if a second building is actually available (see
    Section 7 - this is explicitly flagged there as something to scope
    carefully, not assume).
In every case: report in-distribution AND held-out performance side by
side, never only the flattering one - this directly operationalizes your
brief's instruction to be skeptical and avoid exaggerated claims.

G.1.1 ESP32-C5 / 5GHz SUITABILITY (VERIFIED spec check, Espressif/
distributor product pages):
  The ESP32-C5 is Espressif's first dual-band (2.4GHz AND 5GHz) Wi-Fi 6
  (802.11ax) SoC, with 802.11b/g/n backward compatibility, a single-core
  RISC-V processor up to 240MHz, 400KB on-chip SRAM, BLE 5, Zigbee 3.0,
  Thread 1.4, and critically: 1T1R (single transmit, single receive
  antenna/spatial stream) in BOTH bands. This means the ESP32-C5 CANNOT
  exploit Wi-Fi 6 MU-MIMO or multi-stream OFDMA throughput gains - its
  practical throughput ceiling is set by the single-stream PHY rate, not by
  "Wi-Fi 6" marketing numbers. For PiProbe's purposes (RSSI/channel/
  latency/jitter/loss measurement, not raw throughput maximization), this
  is a non-issue; for any throughput benchmarking claim involving the C5,
  the 1T1R limitation must be stated so reviewers do not expect multi-
  hundred-Mbps single-probe throughput.
  Recommendation: 2.4GHz-only ESP32 probes are SUFFICIENT for the fault
  taxonomy in F.1 (none of the 8 classes inherently requires 5GHz); ESP32-
  C5 is worth including for ONE or two probes specifically to test whether
  5GHz changes interference/coverage fault signatures, but is not required
  core infrastructure for a first paper (see Section 7).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
G.2 SOFTWARE / MEASUREMENT METHODOLOGY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - MQTT (Mosquitto) for probe telemetry; InfluxDB or SQLite for storage;
    Grafana for visualization - standard, as discussed in Section C (not a
    contribution, just the plumbing).
  - Throughput tool: iperf2 vs iperf3 - VERIFIED from the official iperf3
    FAQ (software.es.net), this is not a trivial choice:
      * iperf3 was, for most of its history (pre-v3.16, which only landed
        multi-threading in 2023), SINGLE-THREADED - one thread handles all
        streams. On a resource-constrained embedded device (an ESP32 has
        far less CPU headroom than a PC), a single-threaded tool pegging
        one core can itself become the measurement bottleneck rather than
        the network, contaminating throughput results.
      * iperf2 is multi-threaded (one thread per stream) and was
        specifically noted in community discussion as more appropriate on
        CPU-constrained embedded/router-class hardware for exactly this
        reason.
      * iperf3 is explicitly NOT officially supported on Windows (iperf2
        is) - irrelevant if your Pi/ESP32 side never touches Windows, but
        worth noting if any lab Windows machine is used for comparison
        runs.
      * iperf2 and iperf3 report data in SLIGHTLY different ways and are
        not perfectly cross-compatible; PICK ONE tool and use it
        consistently for every throughput measurement in the dataset -
        mixing iperf2 and iperf3 results in the same dataset without a
        clearly documented conversion/caveat is a reviewer-bait
        methodological inconsistency.
      Recommendation: iperf2, run FROM the Raspberry Pi as the controlled
      server endpoint, with lightweight client-side throughput probes on
      the ESP32s if the ESP32 iperf-equivalent library's CPU overhead is
      budgeted and reported (the ESP32's own CPU load during a throughput
      test is itself a confound worth logging, not ignoring).
  - Time synchronization: use SNTP (ESP-IDF has native SNTP/LwIP support)
    for the ESP32 probes against the Raspberry Pi acting as a local NTP
    reference (chrony/ntpd on the Pi, itself synced to a stratum-1/2
    source over its wired Ethernet link - the Pi's wired connection is
    exactly why it is well-suited to be the local time reference). Report
    the achieved synchronization accuracy (typical LAN-local NTP/SNTP is
    single-digit-to-low-tens-of-milliseconds; this is adequate for the
    second-to-tens-of-seconds granularity of fault-injection windows in
    F.2, but should be explicitly measured and stated, not assumed,
    because it directly bounds how precisely "detection delay" (Section I)
    can be measured.
  - Sampling interval: match or justify against precedent - RAID samples
    every 3 seconds from the access point; WITNESS/multi-modal benchmark's
    exact interval was not confirmed in this search. Recommend 1-5 second
    sampling for PiProbe's probes, stated explicitly, with a note on the
    tradeoff (finer sampling = better fault-onset localization and more
    data per trial, at the cost of more probe CPU/radio duty cycle and
    more storage).
  - IPv4 vs IPv6: test and report BOTH if the deployment ISP provides
    IPv6 (most Indian ISPs increasingly do); DNS-fault injection in
    particular can behave differently under IPv6 (different resolver
    paths, Happy Eyeballs client behavior masking a slow path by falling
    back), so at minimum state which protocol was used for every WAN/DNS
    measurement rather than leaving it ambiguous.
  - Packet-loss and jitter calculation: define precisely in the paper
    (e.g., RFC 3550-style jitter estimation if using RTP/UDP probes, or a
    simple interarrival-time standard deviation if using ICMP/custom
    probes) - "jitter" is used loosely in casual engineering writing and a
    reviewer will want the exact formula.

G.3 ESP32 RSSI ACCURACY AND CALIBRATION - METHODOLOGY FINDINGS (VERIFIED,
multiple independent sources)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - RSSI-to-distance/quality mapping is well documented as NOISY and
    environment-dependent even on dedicated research hardware, not just
    ESP32s: a foundational, highly-cited paper (Dong & Dargie, "Evaluation
    of the reliability of RSSI for indoor localization," ICWCUCA 2012 -
    NOTE: this predates your requested 2014-present window; included here
    as essential grounding context only, flagged as outside the requested
    date range) found RSSI error "unacceptably high" for indoor
    localization without calibration. This is the right framing for
    PiProbe: do not present raw RSSI as reliable; present CALIBRATED RSSI
    with reported residual error.
  - ESP32-specific evidence (2023-2025, within your requested window):
      * A 2025 journal paper (ELKHA: Jurnal Teknik Elektro, Oct 2025)
        evaluating RSSI-based distance estimation with ESP32 BLE modules
        (same radio front-end family, though BLE not Wi-Fi station mode)
        found reliable estimation only within about 4 meters with under
        25% error in clear line-of-sight, using a log-distance path-loss
        model calibrated with a measured reference RSSI at 1 meter (-47
        dBm) and a path-loss exponent of 2; beyond ~5 meters, or with wall
        obstruction, RSSI fluctuated heavily and distance was
        over-estimated. This is directly relevant to PiProbe's coverage-
        fault class: expect RSSI-based coverage estimates to be
        trustworthy only at short range/LOS, and say so.
      * A 2024 arXiv paper on ESP32-S2 Fine Time Measurement (FTM, i.e.
        802.11mc round-trip-time ranging) found that RSSI-based distance
        estimates in an indoor multipath environment were "clearly very
        inaccurate," with RTT/FTM-based estimates substantially more
        accurate (about 75% of FTM-based measurements under 5m error vs
        about 50% for the raw RTT parameter) - suggesting that if the
        target ESP32 variant supports FTM, RTT-based ranging may be a
        BETTER-calibrated complementary signal to RSSI for coverage
        estimation, worth at least discussing even if not adopted as the
        primary signal in a first paper.
      * Indoor-positioning papers using ESP32 RSSI (e.g., a 2023/2024
        arXiv paper combining Wi-Fi+BLE RSSI with Kalman filtering + KNN +
        an RNN/LSTM model) consistently find raw RSSI needs active
        filtering (Kalman filtering is the most common choice in this
        literature) before being fed into any downstream model - PiProbe's
        calibration step should include, at minimum, a documented per-
        device offset correction and a smoothing/filtering stage, not
        raw RSSI straight into the classifier.
  - BOARD-TO-BOARD VARIATION: not covered by a single dedicated ESP32
    paper found in this search, but it is implied and handled-around
    throughout the indoor-positioning literature above (every system that
    gets good results applies SOME form of per-device calibration/
    filtering rather than trusting raw RSSI across units) - treat
    per-device RSSI offset calibration (e.g., co-locating all probes at a
    known reference distance/attenuation before deployment and recording
    each unit's offset from the group mean) as a REQUIRED step, and report
    the measured spread across your specific probe units as an explicit
    number in the paper, since this appears to be an evidence gap in the
    literature that PiProbe can genuinely help fill with real reported
    numbers.
  - ANTENNA ORIENTATION / ENCLOSURE EFFECTS: not covered by a dedicated
    verified source in this pass; recommend PiProbe run and report its own
    simple controlled test (several fixed orientations x several candidate
    enclosures, at a fixed reference distance) BEFORE the main data
    collection, since no external citation was found to lean on here -
    this is a small, cheap, easy sub-experiment that directly strengthens
    the "calibrated" claim in your own title.
  - 2.4GHz LIMITATIONS: three non-overlapping channels (1/6/11 in most
    regulatory domains), high susceptibility to non-Wi-Fi ISM-band
    interference (microwave ovens, Bluetooth, some cordless phones) -
    standard, well-established RF engineering, usable as background/
    motivation without needing a fresh citation.

H. DATASET SCHEMA
================================================================================
Recommended structure (per-sample / per-telemetry-row):

  timestamp_utc          (synchronized per G.2's time-sync procedure)
  probe_id                (unique per physical ESP32/Pi unit - required for
                           cross-device validation, F.4)
  room_id                 (not GPS, per your original design)
  building_id              (for cross-building validation, if applicable)
  link_type               (wifi_probe | wired_pi_baseline)
  wifi_rssi_dbm           (raw)
  wifi_rssi_dbm_calibrated (after per-device offset correction, G.3)
  wifi_channel
  wifi_bssid               (hashed/anonymized if the open dataset is to be
                           publicly released, to avoid exposing real
                           neighboring-network identifiers)
  local_rtt_ms             (probe <-> Pi, wired-baseline-referenced)
  local_jitter_ms          (formula stated explicitly per G.2)
  local_packet_loss_pct
  local_throughput_mbps    (iperf2, per G.2 - note tool version used)
  wan_latency_ms            (Pi -> external reference, multiple targets
                           recommended to avoid single-target bias)
  dns_resolution_ms
  http_response_ms
  wan_throughput_mbps
  ip_version                (v4 | v6, per G.2)
  injected_fault_class      (one of the 8 classes in F.1, or "normal";
                           NULL only during explicitly-excluded transition
                           windows, per F.3 - never silently mislabeled)
  injection_params          (structured: attenuation estimate / netem rule /
                           competing-traffic bitrate / power-cycle event,
                           as applicable to the active fault_class)
  trial_id                  (groups all rows belonging to one F.2 trial)
  measurement_uncertainty_flag (set when a known-noisy condition applies,
                           e.g., sample taken beyond the ~4-5m RSSI-
                           reliability range found in G.3, so downstream
                           users of the open dataset can filter/weight
                           accordingly - this field operationalizes your
                           brief's explicit call for confidence intervals
                           and is, to this search's knowledge, not present
                           in any comparator dataset, including WITNESS)

Train/validation/test split: split by trial_id AND room_id (never by
individual timestamp row) to prevent leakage - rows from the same trial are
highly autocorrelated, and splitting within a trial would let the model
"cheat" by memorizing a trial's specific noise signature rather than
learning the fault signature. For cross-room validation (F.4), the
test-room's data must be entirely absent from both train and validation.

I. EVALUATION PLAN
================================================================================
  - Primary metrics: accuracy, macro-F1 (not micro-F1/accuracy alone,
    given class imbalance between NORMAL and fault classes is expected),
    precision/recall per class, full confusion matrix - directly following
    RAID's own reported metric vocabulary (Section B.8) so PiProbe's
    numbers are legible and comparable to the closest ML precedent.
  - False-ISP-alarm rate: fraction of non-WAN-fault trials (e.g., probe
    faults, Wi-Fi coverage faults) misclassified as "WAN/ISP fault" -
    explicitly named in your brief, explicitly NOT reported by any
    comparator system found in this search; this is a genuinely novel
    reported metric for this literature and should be a headline number in
    the paper's abstract.
  - Detection delay: wall-clock time from true fault onset (per the
    injection log, F.3) to the model's first correct classification,
    bounded below by the time-sync accuracy established in G.2 - report
    both the delay AND the time-sync precision that bounds how finely
    delay can even be measured.
  - Calibration error: for the RSSI-to-coverage-confidence mapping (G.3)
    specifically, report a reliability-diagram-style calibration error
    (e.g., expected calibration error, ECE) between the model's stated
    confidence and its empirical accuracy - this directly operationalizes
    the "calibrated" in your project's own title and should not be
    skipped even under time pressure.
  - Ablations (minimum set): (a) cross-layer fusion vs single-layer-only
    (tests H1), (b) calibrated vs raw RSSI (tests H2), (c) with vs without
    explicit probe-health class (tests H4), (d) ML classifier vs
    simple rule-based/threshold baseline (tests whether the ML layer earns
    its complexity - a rule-based baseline that does nearly as well on the
    "obvious" classes, e.g. probe-power-loss, is a valuable, HONEST finding
    to report, not something to hide).
  - Cost/power/uptime/scalability: report per-unit hardware cost (ESP32 +
    enclosure + power), measured power draw per probe, measured probe
    uptime/failure rate over the full data-collection period (this
    DIRECTLY feeds the probe-fault class's real-world base rate, closing
    the loop between Sections F.1 and I), and a stated/justified estimate
    of how probe count scales (MQTT broker and Pi compute headroom at
    3 vs 6 vs hypothetically more probes).
