TRINETRA
PORTABLE NETWORK INTELLIGENCE AND OUTAGE DIAGNOSIS SYSTEM

Complete project explanation in simple English


1. INTRODUCTION
===============

Trinetra is a portable system that helps us understand why a network is slow,
unstable, or completely unavailable.

Normally, when the Internet stops working, people say that the Wi-Fi is bad.
However, the real problem may be somewhere else. The Wi-Fi signal may be
strong while the Internet service provider is having a problem. The router may
be overloaded. A nearby access point may be using the same wireless channel.
The Raspberry Pi may not be reachable. A mobile network may have a weak signal
even though the phone shows that mobile data is connected.

Trinetra measures these different layers separately. It then brings the results
together and gives an evidence-based explanation of the likely problem.

The system is portable. It is not designed only for one home. We can carry the
Raspberry Pi and ESP32 probes to a college, office, hostel, laboratory, shop,
or another building. At each location, we can create a new survey session and
compare the results.

The main idea is simple:

  Measure the right evidence -> store it -> compare the layers -> explain the fault.


2. THE PROBLEM WE ARE SOLVING
==============================

Network problems are usually described in a very general way:

  - "The Internet is slow."
  - "The Wi-Fi is not working."
  - "The video keeps buffering."
  - "The connection works in one room but not another."

These descriptions do not identify the actual cause.

For example, imagine a user in a classroom. The laptop shows two bars of
Wi-Fi, so the user believes that the router is the problem. In reality, there
may be three different situations:

  Situation 1: The wireless signal is weak in that classroom.
  Situation 2: The wireless signal is good, but the local router is overloaded.
  Situation 3: The Wi-Fi and router are good, but the ISP connection is slow.

All three situations feel similar to the user, but they need different
solutions. Moving an access point will not solve an ISP outage. Changing a
wireless channel will not solve a damaged Ethernet cable. Restarting the
router will not improve a weak mobile signal.

Trinetra solves this identification problem by checking the network in layers.
It measures the wireless environment, the local gateway, the Raspberry Pi,
and the outside Internet path. It can also receive cellular information from
a phone when the project is testing Jio, BSNL, or another mobile carrier.


3. WHAT ALREADY EXISTS
=======================

Several tools already exist, but they normally focus on only one part of the
problem.

3.1 Speed-test applications

Applications such as speed-test tools measure download speed, upload speed,
latency, and sometimes packet loss. They are useful for checking the Internet
path, but they usually cannot tell whether the problem is caused by Wi-Fi,
the router, the ISP, or the device itself.

3.2 Wi-Fi analyzer applications

Wi-Fi analyzer applications show signal strength, nearby access points, and
wireless channels. They are useful for finding coverage holes and interference.
However, they generally do not measure the complete route from the sensor to
the router, from the router to the Raspberry Pi, and from the Pi to the
Internet.

3.3 Router and ISP dashboards

Router dashboards may show connected devices, link speed, or Internet status.
They normally do not measure conditions at several physical points in a
building. They also cannot independently confirm what a user experiences in a
particular room.

3.4 Monitoring platforms

Platforms such as Grafana, Zabbix, and other monitoring systems can display
time-series data. They are powerful, but they require a data source and are
usually configured for fixed infrastructure. They do not automatically give a
portable, simple explanation of a field problem.

3.5 Mobile-network applications

Mobile applications can display carrier name, cell ID, LTE/5G type, RSRP,
RSRQ, and other radio values. However, these applications usually display the
information on the phone. They do not automatically connect the cellular
measurements with the Wi-Fi and WAN measurements collected by Trinetra
system.

Trinetra does not claim that these existing tools are useless. Instead, it
combines their most useful ideas into one portable measurement and explanation
workflow.


4. OUR PROPOSED SOLUTION
========================

Trinetra uses three main types of measurement:

  1. ESP32 Wi-Fi probes measure the local wireless and LAN conditions.
  2. Raspberry Pi measures the WAN and Internet conditions.
  3. A phone companion workflow reports cellular and location information.

The ESP32 probes are placed at different locations. Each probe measures its
Wi-Fi signal and tests the local network path. The probes send their readings
to the Raspberry Pi using MQTT.

The Raspberry Pi acts as the central collector. It receives the MQTT messages,
runs its own WAN tests, and stores all measurements in InfluxDB. The dashboard
reads the stored live data and presents it in a simple form.

Every measurement can include:

  - probe ID;
  - site ID;
  - session ID;
  - location ID;
  - timestamp;
  - Wi-Fi or phone radio values;
  - local network measurements;
  - WAN measurements;
  - fault class when known.

This identity information is important because the system is portable. Data
from a college survey must not be mixed silently with data from a home survey.


5. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
======================

The complete architecture is:

  [ESP32 Probe 1] -----\
  [ESP32 Probe 2] ------> Wi-Fi network -> MQTT broker on Raspberry Pi
  [ESP32 Probe 3] -----/                         |
                                                v
                                      MQTT-to-InfluxDB bridge
                                                |
                                                v
                                          InfluxDB database
                                                |
                    [Raspberry Pi WAN probe] --+
                                                |
                    [Phone telemetry] ----------+
                                                v
                                      Trinetra live dashboard
                                                |
                                                v
                                  Human-readable fault explanation

5.1 ESP32 Wi-Fi probes

The ESP32 is a low-cost wireless measurement device. It connects to the local
Wi-Fi network and periodically measures:

  - received signal strength, called RSSI;
  - round-trip time to the local gateway;
  - round-trip time to the Raspberry Pi;
  - packet loss;
  - nearby access-point counts;
  - channel and BSSID information when available;
  - uptime, free memory, and reconnects.

The ESP32 sends this information as a JSON message over MQTT. A probe can be
given a location such as entrance, library, classroom, or bedroom.

5.2 MQTT broker

Mosquitto is used as the MQTT broker. MQTT is a lightweight messaging protocol
that works well for small devices. It lets the ESP32 publish telemetry without
knowing how the database or dashboard works.

The topics follow this general structure:

  piprobe/<probe_id>/<layer>

Examples include:

  piprobe/esp_probe_1/wifi
  piprobe/pi/wan
  piprobe/phone/telemetry

5.3 Raspberry Pi collector

The Raspberry Pi is the central edge computer. It runs the MQTT broker, the
MQTT-to-InfluxDB bridge, the WAN probe, and the dashboard server.

The WAN probe measures the outside Internet independently of the ESP32. It
checks the gateway, Internet latency, packet loss, DNS response time, and HTTP
response time. This allows the system to compare local Wi-Fi evidence with
outside Internet evidence.

5.4 InfluxDB

InfluxDB stores time-stamped telemetry. Each measurement is saved with fields
and tags such as probe_id, layer, site_id, session_id, and location_id.

InfluxDB is useful because Trinetra needs to answer questions such as:

  - What was the signal in the last five minutes?
  - Did packet loss start at the same time as the outage?
  - Was the WAN slow while Wi-Fi remained healthy?
  - Did the phone signal change when the carrier was changed?

5.5 Dashboard

The live dashboard is served from the Raspberry Pi. It shows current values,
history, probe identity, fault classification, and recovery instructions.

The dashboard is live-only. If the data source is unavailable, it displays a
live error and clears the affected values. It does not replace missing values
with fake demo numbers.


6. HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS STEP BY STEP
=====================================

Step 1: Prepare the survey

The operator connects the Pi and probes to the network being tested. A site,
session, and location identity are selected.

Step 2: Start the Pi services

The Pi starts Mosquitto, InfluxDB, the MQTT bridge, the WAN probe, and the
dashboard. The operator can check the services over SSH without needing the Pi
desktop GUI.

Step 3: ESP32 probes publish measurements

Each ESP32 connects to Wi-Fi and publishes a telemetry message every few
seconds. The message includes the probe identity and measured values.

Step 4: The Pi receives and stores data

The bridge subscribes to Trinetra MQTT topics, validates the numeric fields, and
writes accepted records to InfluxDB. Messages without usable numeric telemetry
are ignored instead of being shown as valid measurements.

Step 5: The Pi performs WAN measurements

The Pi independently tests the local gateway and the Internet. This provides a
second layer of evidence. The Pi can report a healthy local Wi-Fi path even
when Internet latency is poor.

Step 6: The dashboard queries live data

The dashboard discovers available probes and queries the selected time window.
The operator can select an ESP32, the Pi WAN probe, or the phone probe.

Step 7: The diagnosis engine compares layers

The system checks relationships between the measurements. For example:

  - weak RSSI + high gateway delay -> likely Wi-Fi coverage problem;
  - good RSSI + high Internet delay -> likely WAN or ISP problem;
  - good Wi-Fi + high gateway loss -> likely local router or LAN problem;
  - weak phone RSRP + normal Wi-Fi -> likely cellular coverage weakness;
  - no probe messages -> probe, network, MQTT, or power problem.

Step 8: The operator records the result

The dashboard supports history and CSV export. A survey can therefore be
compared across locations, times, carriers, or network configurations.


7. DIFFICULTIES FACED DURING DEVELOPMENT
========================================

7.1 Devices were on different networks

The Pi sometimes used a home Wi-Fi network, while the ESP32 used a phone
hotspot. They had different IP address ranges. The ESP32 could connect to its
Wi-Fi but could not reach the Pi's MQTT broker.

How we tackled it:

  - checked the Pi interfaces and routing table;
  - identified the active Pi address;
  - changed the broker configuration to use the correct network;
  - added portable broker configuration and the piprobe.local option;
  - introduced a setup workflow instead of assuming one permanent IP.

7.2 The Pi could have Internet but no ESP32 telemetry

USB tethering gave the Pi Internet access, but it did not automatically place
the Pi on the same LAN as the ESP32. This made WAN tests work while MQTT from
the ESP32 failed.

How we tackled it:

  - separated Internet access from local MQTT reachability;
  - checked wlan0, USB interfaces, routes, and MQTT listeners;
  - documented that the Pi and ESP32 must share a reachable network;
  - added direct MQTT monitoring commands for troubleshooting.

7.3 The original dashboard used demo data

An early version could display simulated scenarios. This made the interface
look complete even when the real hardware was disconnected. That is dangerous
for a diagnostic system because a user may believe that a made-up number is a
real measurement.

How we tackled it:

  - removed normal demo controls from the live dashboard;
  - added LIVE DATA and LIVE ERROR states;
  - cleared metrics when the source is unavailable;
  - displayed recovery instructions instead of fabricated values;
  - kept the separate demo runner only for offline presentation and testing.

7.4 Phone values were not available through USB tethering alone

USB tethering exposes Internet connectivity, not necessarily the phone's
carrier, GPS, SIM slot, cell ID, RSRP, RSRQ, or SINR.

How we tackled it:

  - used Termux and Termux:API as a free phone companion workflow;
  - collected carrier, network type, cell, location, RSRP, and RSRQ values;
  - accepted that SINR and SIM slot may be unavailable;
  - showed missing values as Unavailable instead of guessing.

7.5 GPS did not always return a fix

Indoor GPS can return no result even when network location works.

How we tackled it:

  - used the network location provider when satellite GPS was unavailable;
  - removed unsupported command options;
  - recorded provider and accuracy so the source of the location is clear.

7.6 Database versions were inconsistent

The project had code using InfluxDB 1.x queries and a Docker file using
InfluxDB 2.x buckets and Flux. This could make the database receive data while
the dashboard showed no data.

How we tackled it:

  - selected InfluxDB 1.8/InfluxQL as the canonical prototype architecture;
  - aligned Docker, Telegraf, bridge, and dashboard configuration;
  - added deployment smoke tests to detect the mismatch.

7.7 Credentials were present in development files

Wi-Fi and other passwords can accidentally be copied into firmware source,
chat messages, or reports.

How we tackled it:

  - removed the local Wi-Fi credential from the shared project configuration;
  - added safe configuration templates;
  - documented credential rotation and private configuration rules;
  - kept hardware secrets outside the public project files.

7.8 The first service and startup files were inconsistent

Some files used an older project name and dangling symbolic links. The service
installer expected one filename while the folder contained another.

How we tackled it:

  - created real Trinetra service and startup files;
  - removed stale Trinetra startup links;
  - added dependency-free smoke checks for required files and paths.


8. HOW TRINETRA IS BETTER FOR THIS PROJECT
==========================================

Trinetra is not better than every existing network tool in every situation. Its
advantage is the combination of measurements and the explanation workflow.

8.1 Cross-layer diagnosis

Most simple tools measure either Wi-Fi or Internet speed. Trinetra compares the
wireless layer, local gateway, Pi path, WAN path, and optional cellular layer.

8.2 Portable operation

Site, session, and location IDs allow the same system to be carried to a new
place. The operator can compare two buildings or two carriers using the same
measurement structure.

8.3 Low cost

The system uses Raspberry Pi and ESP32 hardware rather than expensive network
measurement appliances. Open protocols such as MQTT and InfluxDB are used.

8.4 Explainable results

The dashboard does not only show graphs. It connects the measurements to a
likely fault class and explains which evidence supports the conclusion.

8.5 Honest missing-data behavior

Trinetra distinguishes between zero, unavailable, stale, and failed data. This
is important because a missing measurement must not be interpreted as a good
measurement.

8.6 Extensible design

New probes, phone agents, sensors, or classifiers can publish telemetry using
the same identity and MQTT structure.

10. LIMITATIONS
===============

  - The current ML evaluation uses development data and must be confirmed with
    a real held-out field dataset.
  - Android may not expose every cellular field on every device.
  - GPS can be unavailable indoors.
  - Wi-Fi and phone hotspot behavior depends on the physical location and
    operating-system settings.
  - MQTT anonymous mode is acceptable only for a trusted prototype LAN; a
    public or untrusted deployment needs authentication and encryption.
  - The system identifies likely causes from evidence. It does not guarantee
    that a classifier has found the only possible cause.

12. CONCLUSION
==============

Trinetra is a low-cost portable network diagnosis system. Its main contribution
is not just collecting Wi-Fi or Internet numbers. It combines several layers
of evidence and explains what those layers mean together.

The ESP32 shows what is happening near the user. The Raspberry Pi shows what
is happening at the network boundary and on the Internet path. The phone can
show cellular and location evidence. MQTT connects the devices, InfluxDB keeps
the history, and the dashboard turns the measurements into a practical
diagnosis.

The project faced real problems involving network routing, changing IP
addresses, unavailable phone permissions, missing GPS fixes, database-version
mismatches, stale data, and exposed development credentials. These problems
were handled by making the system portable, live-only, explicit about missing
data, configurable, and testable.

The final system is intended to answer a practical question:

  When a network fails at this location, which layer is actually responsible?

That question is the central purpose of Trinetra.
